Train Like It's Real

Why the teams that handle incidents well have usually handled them before — and what realistic, hands-on practice actually takes.

Radharaman SethiOct 12, 20268 min read
🎯

It's 2:47 a.m. The on-call analyst is looking at a ransom note on a file server, three workstations beaconing to an address nobody recognises, and a message from her manager asking whether they should shut the network down.

She knows this material. She has the certifications, she's read the incident response plan, and she could sketch the attack chain on a whiteboard without thinking. What she doesn't have is the memory of having done it before. Which host do you isolate first? Which logs actually matter right now, and which can wait? How long do you investigate before you pull the plug and accept the downtime?

Anyone who has worked in a SOC has seen some version of this night. It rarely goes wrong because people don't know enough. It goes wrong because they have never practised under conditions that feel like the real thing.

Knowing it isn't the same as doing it

There's a difference between knowing what lateral movement is and spotting it in a noisy authentication log at three in the morning. One is recall. The other is pattern recognition, prioritisation and judgement, all done with incomplete information while someone senior waits for an answer.

The second kind of skill only comes from repetition. Pilots don't log their first engine failure in a real aircraft. They log it in a simulator, many times, until the response is automatic. Security work deserves the same discipline, but most teams still get their first real practice during a real incident.

The industry is feeling the consequences. In ISC2's 2025 Cybersecurity Workforce Study, which surveyed more than 16,000 professionals, 88% said their organisation had experienced a significant security incident because of a skills shortage, and 69% had experienced more than one. A quarter said they don't have the time or resources to train their staff at all. When training time is that scarce, it can't be spent on formats that don't transfer to the job.

Why slides and scripts stop short

Presentations and theory have their place. Nobody should be configuring a firewall without understanding stateful inspection first, and a good lecture is still the fastest way to build a mental model.

Scripted simulations go a step further, and they're useful for onboarding. But they share a structural weakness: the path is fixed. There is one correct sequence of clicks, the environment behaves exactly as designed, and nothing unexpected happens unless the author planned it. Real networks are messier. Services misbehave, logs are incomplete, and an attacker will ignore the route you prepared for.

The other gap is consequence. In a simulation, a bad decision costs you a point. On a real network, a bad firewall rule takes down a service, an aggressive scan trips an IDS, and a missed persistence mechanism means the attacker is back an hour later. Those consequences are what turn a lesson into instinct.

The business case, in rupees

For decision-makers, the argument for realistic practice is also a financial one. IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report 2026 puts the average breach in India at ₹25.5 crore, up 15.9% on the previous year.

One detail in that report deserves more attention than it gets. Of the cost-reducing measures IBM examined, offensive security testing, such as red teaming and penetration testing, was associated with the largest average saving: ₹2.47 crore per breach. The same study found that 67% of Indian organisations plan to invest further in incident response planning and testing.

The message is hard to miss. Practising the fight, before it's real, is one of the better investments a security team can make.

What a good cyber range gives you

That kind of practice needs somewhere to happen. You can't rehearse a ransomware response on your production network, and you shouldn't hand a junior analyst live exploit tooling against systems the business depends on. That's the gap a cyber range fills: a platform where teams train on live networks that behave like the environments they defend, kept separate from production systems.

Having spent my working life on the infrastructure side of ranges, here's what I think separates a useful one from a glorified slideshow.

Real systems, not recordings

Participants should work on real virtual machines running real operating systems, connected over routed networks and protected by the same categories of equipment they meet at work: firewalls, routers, VPN gateways, load balancers, IDS/IPS sensors and web application firewalls. For teams defending factories or utilities, the best ranges reach further, to mobile targets and even physical IoT, OT and SCADA devices.

Scenarios built around real, publicly disclosed vulnerabilities are especially valuable. Rather than reading an advisory and nodding along, a team can exploit the flaw on one side of the range and detect and patch it on the other. That's a far better way to understand why a CVE matters.

Getting in shouldn't be the hard part

Access gets less attention than it deserves. If it takes twenty minutes to reach a lab, or the console fights you at every step, the exercise is lost before it starts. Browser-based SSH, RDP and VNC with working copy and paste, plus VPN access for people who want to bring their own toolkit, remove that friction. Access should be per user, so activity stays attributable.

An adversary that doesn't need a lunch break

Blue-team training has an awkward dependency: someone has to attack. Good red teamers are scarce and expensive. Automated attack engines that run real exploit chains against defenders while the exercise is live, with each step mapped to MITRE ATT&CK techniques, fill that seat. The mapping matters afterwards too. When a team misses something, the debrief isn't "you missed an attack". It's "you didn't catch credential dumping, and here's the technique to watch for", which is feedback a team can take straight back to its detection rules.

Evidence to work with

Think about the analyst at the start. Most of her night would be spent in logs, and log analysis simply can't be learned from a slide. A range should collect logs from targets, network devices, security appliances and the players' own workstations, and let teams search and correlate them centrally, just as they would in a SIEM at work.

Mistakes are the point

Here's the part most training programmes skip: people learn fastest from the things they get wrong.

In production, nobody tries an aggressive containment strategy just to see what happens. On a range, that's exactly what you should do. Block the wrong subnet, watch the application fall over, work out why, and try again. Labs that reset to a clean state on demand mean a failed attempt doesn't cost a day of rebuilding.

Mistakes only teach if someone looks at them, though. Live instructor visibility, full activity logging and a proper debrief are where the real learning lands. That's the moment a team sees its own timeline and realises it spent forty minutes on a host that didn't matter.

The most useful thing a range offers isn't the technology. It's permission to fail where failing is cheap.

Real enough to matter, separate enough to be safe

The obvious concern with live-fire training is containment. If you give people working exploits and administrative access, what stops something leaking? The answer has to be built into the infrastructure: separate environments per organisation and per exercise, role-based access, and a platform designed on the assumption that participants will attack it, because on a range that's their job. I've written about what that means for OpenStack isolation in more detail.

Choosing the right kind of pressure

Not every team needs the same kind of pressure. Capture the Flag challenges sharpen individual technical skills. King of the Hill adds the need to defend a system you've just taken while others try to take it from you. Drills are guided, repeatable scenarios run until the playbook becomes habit. Team-based red-versus-blue exercises test what usually breaks first in a real incident: communication, handovers and decision-making across a group.

A sensible progression starts with CTFs and drills and builds toward full team exercises as confidence grows.

The first time shouldn't be the real time

Go back to that analyst at 2:47 a.m. Suppose she had worked through a ransomware drill three times this quarter, on a network that behaved like hers. The night would still be stressful. But she would know which host to isolate first, what the logs should look like, and what to tell her manager. Her hands would already know the work.

That's the case for training like it's real. Attackers rehearse all the time. Defenders deserve the same chance, somewhere it is safe to fail. 🎯
