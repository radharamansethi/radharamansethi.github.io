Why I Started Learning in Public

Writing about what I'm learning — before I feel like an expert — has been the single biggest accelerator for my growth as an engineer.

Radharaman SethiApr 18, 20265 min read
🚀

For a long time, my learning lived in private places: a notes app with 400 untitled files, a folder of half-finished side projects, and browser tabs I swore I'd read "later". I was learning a lot. I just had nothing to show for it — and, if I'm honest, I wasn't retaining much of it either.

So I tried something that felt uncomfortable: writing about what I'm learning while I'm still learning it. Not after I've mastered it. Not once I feel qualified. Now.

What "learning in public" actually means

It doesn't mean live-streaming every keystroke or pretending to be an expert. For me it's simple:

  • When I figure something out, I write a short note explaining it.
  • When I build something, I push it somewhere people can see it.
  • When I'm stuck, I say so — and share how I got unstuck.

That's it. This blog is the most visible part of that habit.

Why it works (for me, at least)

1. Explaining exposes the gaps

I thought I understood the JavaScript event loop until I tried to write an article about it. Three paragraphs in, I realised I couldn't explain why a Promise callback runs before a setTimeout with zero delay. Writing forced me to go and actually find out.

If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough — and writing is the fastest way to find out which one you are.

2. Future-me is a grateful reader

Half the traffic to my own notes is from me. Six months after debugging a gnarly Docker build, I don't remember the fix — but I remember that I wrote it down.

3. It builds a trail of proof

A CV says "familiar with Docker". A blog post walking through how you cut an image size by 80% shows it. Public work turns vague claims into concrete evidence.

4. You meet people you'd never meet otherwise

Every time you share something, there's a chance someone more experienced replies with "nice — have you tried X?" Those little nudges compound.

The fear (and why it's mostly wrong)

The biggest blocker was the voice that says: "Who are you to write about this? Someone has already explained it better."

Two things helped me get past it:

  1. You're writing for the person one step behind you. Experts often forget what it's like not to know something. You still remember — that's an advantage.
  2. Being wrong in public is survivable. If I get something wrong, someone corrects me, I update the post, and I learn faster than I would have alone.
💡

A useful framing: you're not publishing a textbook, you're publishing your lab notebook. Notebooks are allowed to be messy.

How I keep it sustainable

  • Small beats perfect. A 400-word post that ships beats a 4,000-word draft that doesn't.
  • Write when it's fresh. The best time to write about a bug is the hour after you fix it.
  • Keep a "things I learned" list. Every item is a potential post.
  • Own the platform. This site is plain HTML on GitHub Pages — no algorithm decides whether my notes get seen.

Your turn

If you've been sitting on a folder of notes, pick the smallest one and publish it this week. It doesn't need to be good. It needs to exist.

And if you do — send it my way. I'd genuinely love to read it. 🚀
